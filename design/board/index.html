Terminal manager · round 2 · design 09

Activity feed

One window that hosts every Claude Code session and terminal, organised around a single live stream of what they're doing. Every session's tool calls, questions, finished turns and your own replies land in one reverse-chronological feed, with a pinned "Waiting on you" lane above it so blocking items never scroll away.

It suits someone running five or more Claude sessions in parallel who mostly supervises: you glance at the feed to see who's progressing, answer what's blocked, and only dive into a full session when you need to steer it. Clicking any session in the left list swaps the feed for that session's full view; "All activity" brings the feed back, and it has kept recording the whole time.

How it answers the brief

  • A list of all sessions on the left. Grouped Claude / Outside the app / Terminals / Recently closed, each row with a status shape, status word, elapsed time and a "new" count.
  • Click one to get into it and use it. The centre becomes that session: transcript, composer, permission prompts, changed-file review, lifecycle controls.
  • Always clear what's ready and what isn't. Header counts, the Waiting on you lane, status shapes that differ by form not just colour, and ⌘J to jump straight to the next thing that needs you.

Main screen, annotated

The default view at 1440×900 in styling a (Lavender). Three columns: the session list, the cross-session feed, and a tray of live terminals. Nothing here is a separate window.

Key states

Each state shown as it appears on screen, cropped where a close-up explains it better than the whole window.

A session that needs you: permission prompt, and the quick answer from the row

Quick answer from the list (left)

Hovering or keyboard-focusing a "Needs you" row unfolds the prompt right under it with its options. "Allow once" is filled because it's the safest yes; Always allow and Deny sit beside it. You never leave the feed, which is the point: permission prompts are usually a two-second decision.

The row's sub-line always carries the status word ("Needs you · Wants to run npm run build"), so the state reads in greyscale too.

Inside the session (right)

Clicking api-server swaps the centre to its full view. The transcript ends on the pending tool call, and the prompt docks at the bottom where the composer normally sits, because nothing else can happen until it's answered. Number keys 1–3 pick an option.

What happens next: the choice is echoed as > Allow once, the status flips to Working with a live timer, the tool output streams in, and the session lands at Ready for review. The matching ask in the feed is stamped "Allowed once" so the history stays honest.

A question with "Type an answer"

mailer asks something Claude can't infer: 7:00 or 8:00? The preset answers are buttons; "Type an answer" is a button too, and pressing it opens a text field under the options, pre-focused, without hiding them, so you can still change your mind and click 8:00.

What happens next

Enter sends. Your answer appears in the transcript as > 8:00, but skip weekends and in the feed as a dashed "From you" event, then the session works and replies. The same field exists on the lane card and in the row's quick answer, so free-text answers never require opening the session.

Talking to a Claude session

A three-step storyboard on web-app, from typing a message to the reply landing.

1

Compose. The composer sits under the transcript. Enter sends, Shift+Enter adds a line. Because web-app is Ready, the composer lives inside the review panel and doubles as "Send feedback".

2

Working. Your line is echoed as > …, the status pill turns blue with a live elapsed timer (0:06), and Claude Code's own ✻ Working… (6s · esc to interrupt) line sits at the bottom of the transcript. Stop becomes available in the header. The feed records "You replied" at the same moment.

3

Reply streams in. About three seconds later the reply arrives line by line; each ⏺ line also becomes a feed event. When the turn ends the status settles on Ready for review and, because you talked to it, a toast says so (see Notifications below). The "All activity" button counts what the feed gathered meanwhile ("4 new").

Ready for review: changed files, Looks good, Send feedback

A Ready session shows a review panel instead of a bare composer: every file the turn touched with its additions and removals (binary files labelled as such), a one-line total, and two ways out.

Looks good

Marks the turn reviewed. The session drops out of the Waiting on you lane and its row turns to a green check with the word "Reviewed", a quiet state that means "nothing owed". It doesn't send anything to Claude.

Send feedback

The composer inside the panel. Sending puts the session back to Working exactly like any other message.

Shells: a running process, the command line, and an exited shell

Running process (left)

dev server is running npm run dev. Its command line is replaced by a plain note ("npm run dev is running") and a Stop (⌃C) button, because typing into a server's stdin is rarely what you mean. ⌃C works too. After stopping, ^C prints, the prompt returns and the status becomes Idle.

Terminal tray (right)

Each shell is a collapsible card showing its last line. Expanded, zsh has a live command line: Enter echoes the command after the prompt and prints its output. The exited pytest shell gets a hollow red square, the words "Exited · code 1", and Restart (re-runs pytest) or Remove (moves it to Recently closed).

New session, step by step

⌘N (or the header button) opens one sheet that unfolds in order. Step 1 picks the kind; picking Terminal skips step 3 and creates a zsh at the chosen folder. Step 2 offers recent directories as chips and a typed path that validates as you type. Step 3, Claude only, takes an optional first prompt and a permission mode with a one-line explanation of each. Create adds the session to the list and opens it; with a first prompt it starts Working immediately.

Delete, Recently closed, Resume

Confirmation (left)

Delete always asks, in-page, and says plainly what happens: "Ends the process. Claude sessions can be resumed later from Recently closed." The button names the consequence ("End and delete") rather than "OK". Rename, Restart and Stop sit next to Delete in the session header and never ask.

Recently closed (right)

Deleted api-server moves to the bottom group with "just now". Claude sessions get Resume, which brings them back as Ready with the conversation intact; terminals get Reopen, a fresh zsh in the same folder. The feed keeps a "Deleted" event so the stream still explains where it went.

A session running outside the app

scheduler was started in Terminal.app, not here, so the app doesn't own its process. It learns about it through Claude Code hooks: status changes, tool calls and permission prompts arrive as events, so scheduler appears in the feed and the lane like anything else.

What you can do

Answer its permission prompts and questions (the hook relays your choice), read its activity, rename its label, toggle its bell, and "Show its window" to bring the Terminal.app window forward.

What you can't

Type to it, restart, stop or delete it. Rather than greying those out with no reason, the header simply doesn't show them, and a dashed banner explains why. It sits in its own "Outside the app" group in the list with a Terminal.app badge.

Notifications and ⌘J

Toasts

When a session you talked to finishes its turn, a small toast appears over the feed's bottom-left: "web-app is ready for review" with an Open button. Sessions you haven't touched don't toast; their changes are already visible in the lane and the feed.

Bell and Do not disturb

Every session header has a bell; muting it shows a struck-through bell on its list row so you remember why it's quiet. The header's Do not disturb switch silences all toasts without hiding anything in the feed.

⌘J: next needing you

Jumps through the queue in order of who's waited longest: needs-you sessions first, then ready ones.

mailer 6mthenapi-server 2mthenscheduler 1mthenweb-app 11m

When the queue is empty, ⌘J says "Nothing needs you" in a toast instead of doing nothing.

Three stylings

Same layout and same moment in time, three treatments. A segmented control in the header switches between them; ?style=a|b|c picks one by URL.

Features of its own

Two ideas that only make sense because everything is already one stream of events.

1. Since you last looked

Problem. With six sessions, coming back from a coffee means scrolling a feed of forty events to reconstruct who did what. The feed is great at "now" and bad at "while I was away".

What it does. A card at the top of the feed rolls up each session's events since you last looked at it: "2 edits, 1 command" plus its state now ("waiting on a permission"). Sessions with nothing new are left out. Each line opens that session.

How "looked" is defined. Per session: opening a session marks it seen. "Mark all seen" resets everything. Unseen events in the stream also get a small blue dot, and list rows show "3 new".

2. Pins and event-type filters

Problem. Useful one-off lines get buried: the preview URL web-app printed, the bug scheduler said it found, the test count chess-app reported. In a terminal they scroll away; in a feed they scroll away faster.

Pins. Hovering any event reveals a pin. Pinned events stay in a small strip above the stream until you unpin them, across all sessions, so the feed doubles as a scratch board of things to come back to.

Filters. Chips narrow the stream to Asks, Edits, Commands, Finished or From you, each with a count. "Edits" across six sessions is a fast way to see which files are changing before you review anything.

Motion

  • New feed events slide in at the top with a one-second tint that fades, so your eye catches arrivals without anything blinking.
  • Lane cards collapse sideways when answered, which reads as "handled" and keeps the remaining cards stable.
  • The working shape is a slowly spinning ring; it's the only perpetual motion on screen, so "something is running" is visible from across the room.
  • Reduced motion replaces the spinner with a dotted ring and the slide-ins with instant appearance.

Noticing status changes

  • Header counts update in place; the lane gains or loses a card; the list re-sorts so needs-you rows float to the top of their group.
  • Each status has its own shape and word: diamond Needs you, spinning ring Working, dot Ready, check Reviewed, triangle Running, ring Idle, square Exited.
  • Toasts only for sessions you talked to, never during Do not disturb, never for muted bells.

Trade-offs

Strengths

  • Supervising is cheap. You can watch six sessions progress without opening any of them, and answer most prompts from the lane or a row.
  • History is free. The feed is an audit log: what ran, what you allowed, when it finished.
  • Cross-session questions get answers. "Which sessions touched files?" is one filter chip.
  • Outside sessions fit naturally, since hooks deliver exactly the events a feed is made of.

Weaknesses

  • Interleaving is noisy. Consecutive events from one session are grouped, but a busy feed still splits each session's story into fragments.
  • Two places to look. The feed summarises; real work happens in the session view. Some people will want the transcript always visible.
  • The lane competes for height on smaller screens; above four cards it scrolls sideways, which hides some.

With 15+ sessions

  • The list stays usable because it sorts by status within groups; Reviewed and Idle rows could collapse into a "quiet" fold.
  • The feed gets loud. Read-tool events should collapse into "read 6 files" and a per-session mute should hide a chatty session from the feed without silencing its asks.
  • The lane becomes a count plus the oldest three cards; ⌘J does the rest.

Decide before building

  • Event granularity: which hook events become feed rows, and which collapse or stay hidden.
  • How long the feed persists: this run only, or a day of history across app restarts.
  • Whether "seen" is per session (as drawn) or a single global checkpoint.
  • Whether the terminal tray stays a third column or becomes a drawer to give the feed more width.
